- What specific problem are you solving? (Required, max 200 words, DRAFT v2 — sharpened with referral-tracking gap): "Family caregivers who support a relative with a chronic condition are often responsible for turning a clinician's spoken or written instructions into concrete next steps: booking a follow-up appointment, obtaining a referral, collecting test results before a visit, or preparing questions to ask. These instructions are usually given briefly during an appointment and then tracked informally afterward — in memory, on paper, or across scattered text messages — with no single place recording what was asked for and whether it has been completed. Referrals are a particularly weak point: once a specialist issues one, there is typically no way to see whether it was ever booked or completed, especially when it passes through a separate referral unit. When a caregiver manages instructions from more than one specialist, administrative steps can be missed, delayed, or duplicated. I have not yet confirmed this through caregiver interviews (see 'what I've built and tested'); it is a hypothesis grounded in how fragmented chronic-care coordination is known to be and in a competitor scan that found no existing caregiver app tracking referral status end-to-end. Validating it with real caregivers is my top near-term priority."
- What is your solution? (Required, max 200 words, DRAFT v2 — sharpened with persistent-vs-one-shot differentiation): "CareLoop Zero is a web application that helps a family caregiver keep every clinician-directed follow-up task for one person's chronic care in a single, ongoing place — including tracking a referral's status over time, not just the appointment it eventually produces. A caregiver can manually log a task with a title, an optional due date, and which clinician or appointment it came from, and see it on a dashboard organized by upcoming, overdue, completed, or 'no date known.' Its AI-assisted task drafting lets a caregiver paste a short, non-identifying clinician instruction and get back an editable list of administrative tasks extracted from that text. The AI is deliberately constrained: it only extracts what is explicitly stated, is instructed never to invent a date, diagnosis, medication, or treatment detail, and flags ambiguous text for clarification instead of guessing. No AI-suggested task is saved until the caregiver reviews and approves it individually. Unlike one-shot tools that summarize a single discharge document, CareLoop Zero persists across multiple visits and specialists over time. The current version is a working prototype (Python/Flask, SQLite, a real LLM integration) tested end-to-end with synthetic clinician instructions."

## Page 4: More About Your Solution and Business Model

- What makes your solution innovative? (Required, max 300 words, DRAFT): "Most caregiver apps I found (Carer's Notebook, Caring Village, Medisafe, Carely) focus on medication reminders, shared calendars, and general to-do lists, not on tracking the status of a specific clinician referral as it moves through separate departments or specialists over time — in my research, no surveyed app does this end-to-end. The closest technical analog to my AI feature is Discharge Buddy, a hackathon project that extracts tasks from a single discharge summary; CareLoop Zero differs by being a persistent, ongoing dashboard across multiple visits and specialists rather than a one-time extraction tool, and by requiring the caregiver to individually review and approve every AI-suggested task rather than trusting the AI's output outright. If the underlying problem is validated, the broader contribution is less about the AI mechanic itself (which is not unprecedented) and more about applying a structured, auditable, human-approved task-extraction workflow specifically to the overlooked referral-tracking gap in chronic-care coordination, and demonstrating this can be built safely, without the AI ever inventing clinical details. I see this as more evolutionary than revolutionary: a focused, carefully-scoped tool for an underserved part of an existing workflow, not a reinvention of care coordination."

- Describe how/why you expect impact (Theory of Change) (Required, max 300 words, DRAFT): "Activities: a caregiver logs clinician-directed tasks (manually or via AI-assisted drafting from pasted instructions) and tracks their status on a single dashboard. Immediate outputs: a complete, accurate, centrally visible list of what a clinician asked for, including referral status, instead of information scattered across memory, paper, and messages. Near-term outcome: the caregiver has a clearer view of what remains to be done and is less likely to miss or delay a specific administrative step like booking a referral appointment. Longer-term outcome (hypothesized, not yet evidenced): fewer missed or delayed follow-up steps could mean fewer disruptions to a patient's care continuity. I do not yet have evidence for any of these links beyond the internal logic of the design — no caregiver interviews, no pilot data, and no third-party research specific to my exact mechanism yet reviewed. My immediate next step, independent of Solve's outcome, is testing the first two links in this chain — whether centralizing tasks measurably reduces missed/delayed steps for real caregivers — before making any claim about the longer-term outcome."

- Describe your tech stack and how you know it works (Required, max 300 words, DRAFT): "The current prototype is a Python/Flask backend with SQLite storage, and a plain HTML/CSS/JavaScript frontend with no build step or framework dependency. The AI-assisted task-drafting feature calls a hosted large language model (currently Groq's API, model openai/gpt-oss-120b) through a constrained system prompt that only extracts tasks explicitly stated in the pasted text, is instructed never to invent a date, diagnosis, medication, or treatment detail, and must flag ambiguous input instead of guessing. I know it works because I tested it end-to-end with a synthetic example: the AI correctly extracted three concrete tasks (schedule a cardiology appointment, bring recent blood test and EKG results, obtain a physical therapy referral form) from a short synthetic clinician instruction, and correctly declined to invent a specific due date when the source text only gave a relative timeframe ('within 3 weeks'). I also verified the stack behaves correctly without an AI key configured (a clean, non-crashing error state), and tested manual task creation, editing, completion, and deletion directly in the browser. This is still a single-session, single-developer test on synthetic data, not a load test or a multi-user test, and I have not yet tested the AI's behavior across a larger or more diverse set of real clinician language."
